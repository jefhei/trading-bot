## What Is a Trailing Stop?

A trailing stop is a risk management tool that automatically adjusts your "exit price" (the price at which you sell) as a stock moves in your favor. Unlike a fixed stop-loss — which stays at one price — a trailing stop *moves up* with the stock, locking in more profit over time while still protecting you from big losses.

---

## How It Works (From the PDF)

The transcript walks through a clear example:

You buy a stock at $100 and tell Claude: if this drops to $95, sell it. That's your floor — $5 is the most you're willing to risk on this trade.

Now if the stock climbs to $110, your floor is still sitting at $95, meaning you're exposed to a $15 fall. So instead, you want Claude to move the floor up to $105 — if the stock dips and hits the new floor, you're still up $5 in profit and protected the entire time.

If the stock drops right out of the gate and hits $95, you sell immediately, take the $5 loss, and still have your remaining capital to find the next opportunity.

Ideally, the stock keeps climbing and every time it does, Claude drags the floor higher and higher — you can never fall back to where you started. It eventually sells at $110, giving you a 10% gain.

## The Ladder Buy Addition

The transcript adds another layer on top of the trailing stop:

If the stock drops by over 20–30%, Claude buys more shares — for example, 10 more shares at a 20% drop, 20 more shares at a 30% drop. This way you're getting better prices on the way down instead of just losing money.

This is called **dollar-cost averaging** or **ladder buying** — you're accumulating more shares at cheaper prices, lowering your average cost basis.

## Important Considerations

- **It doesn't guarantee profit.** If a stock drops immediately after purchase and never recovers, the stop loss triggers and you take the loss.
- **It works best in trending markets.** In highly volatile or sideways markets, trailing stops can trigger prematurely before a stock rebounds.
- **The percentage thresholds matter.** Setting them too tight (e.g., 2%) can cause frequent early exits; too loose (e.g., 30%) exposes you to large losses.
- **This is demonstrated using paper trading** (fake money), so real-world results may differ.
