## What Is the Wheel Strategy?

The Wheel Strategy is an options-based income strategy where you act as the **insurance company** rather than the insurance buyer. Instead of paying premiums, you **collect** them at every stage. The name comes from the fact that the strategy cycles continuously — like a spinning wheel — generating income regardless of whether the stock goes up, down, or sideways.

---

## Understanding Options First

Before diving into the strategy itself, the transcript uses a helpful analogy to explain options:

### The Insurance Analogy

| Insurance | Options |
|---|---|
| Pay $100/month premium | Pay premium to another trader |
| Right to file a claim if something goes wrong | Right to buy/sell a stock at a locked price |
| If you crash, insurance covers it | If stock moves your way, you profit |
| If nothing happens, they keep your $100 | If stock doesn't move your way, contract expires |

### The Two Types of Options

**Call Option**
- Gives you the right to **buy** a stock at a locked-in price (strike price)
- Example: Apple is at $200. You buy a call with a $210 strike price. If Apple rises to $230, you can buy it at $210 and pocket the $20 difference. If Apple stays below $210, the contract expires and you lose only the premium paid.

**Put Option**
- Gives you the right to **sell** a stock at a locked-in price
- Example: You own Apple at $200. You buy a put with a $190 strike price. If Apple drops to $170, you can still sell at $190, protecting yourself from the drop.

### Selling Options (The Key Insight)
When you **sell** options rather than buy them, you flip the table and become the insurance company:
- Someone pays **you** a premium upfront
- If the stock doesn't hit the strike price by expiration, the contract dies and you keep everything
- Insurance companies make billions this way — collecting premiums from millions of people and paying out on only a small percentage of claims
- The math works in your favor over time

## The Two Stages of the Wheel

### Stage 1 – Selling Cash-Secured Puts

**Goal:** Get paid to agree to buy a stock you already want, at a price you're happy with.

**How it works (Tesla example from transcript):**
- Tesla is trading at $250
- You'd rather buy it at $230
- You sell a put option at the $230 strike price
- Someone hands you $5 per share = **$500 in your pocket immediately**

**Two possible outcomes:**

| Outcome | What Happens | Result |
|---|---|---|
| Tesla stays above $230 | Contract expires worthless | You keep the $500 and repeat |
| Tesla drops below $230 | You must buy 100 shares | You buy at an effective cost of $225 (because you already collected $500) |

The key insight: if you get assigned (forced to buy), you actually got the stock at a **discount** — $225 instead of $230 — because of the premium you collected upfront. And you wanted it anyway.

---

### Stage 2 – Selling Covered Calls

**Goal:** Generate income from shares you already own, while setting a profitable exit price.

**How it works (continuing the Tesla example):**
- You now own 100 Tesla shares at an effective cost of $225
- You sell a covered call at a $260 strike price
- Someone pays you another $5 per share = **another $500 collected**

**Two possible outcomes:**

| Outcome | What Happens | Result |
|---|---|---|
| Tesla stays below $260 | Contract expires worthless | Keep shares + keep $500, sell another call |
| Tesla rises above $260 | Your shares get sold at $260 | Lock in gains and go back to Stage 1 |

**If shares get called away, here's the full math:**

| Source | Amount |
|---|---|
| Stock gain ($225 cost → $260 sale) | $35/share |
| Premium from selling the put | $5/share |
| Premium from selling the covered call | $5/share |
| **Total profit** | **$45/share** |
| **On 100 shares** | **$4,500** |

Once the shares are sold, you go **right back to Stage 1** — sell another put, collect another premium, and the wheel keeps spinning.

## Risk Considerations

### The Main Risk: A Stock That Keeps Falling
The biggest danger with the wheel strategy is if the stock you're assigned drops significantly and keeps falling. For example:
- You're assigned Tesla at $225
- Tesla drops to $150 and stays there
- Your covered calls may not generate enough premium to offset the loss
- You're stuck holding a depreciating stock

This is why the transcript emphasizes **only running the wheel on stocks you actually want to own** at the price you're targeting.

### Other Risks to Keep in Mind

| Risk | Description |
|---|---|
| **Assignment risk** | You must have enough cash to buy shares if put is exercised |
| **Opportunity cost** | If stock rockets up past your call strike, gains are capped |
| **Volatility risk** | Very volatile stocks make strike/expiration selection harder |
| **Early assignment** | American-style options can be exercised before expiration |
| **Liquidity risk** | Low-volume options may have wide bid-ask spreads |
